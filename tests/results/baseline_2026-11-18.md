## What the two arms actually produce

- **With retrieval:** 6/6 cases pass every check (18/18 individual checks). 13 recommendations were made, 13 of them carry a source link; 36 documented constraint rules were applied; 23 application windows were read.
- **Without retrieval:** 0/6 cases pass (9/18 checks, of which 9 are vacuous - they pass only because there is nothing to inspect). 0 recommendations, 0 citations, 0 constraint rules, 0 windows.

A concrete example of the difference, verbatim from the run:

> **Case C1:** _I am a Year 2 student interested in data science. What should I study next, and which research or internship options should I investigate?_

> With retrieval - Found 2 course option(s) that pass the documented checks and 1 currently open opportunity lead(s) for a Year 2 student in S1.

> Without retrieval - For a Year 2 student interested in data science, a reasonable S1 plan is to take an advanced course in data science together with a supporting statistics or database course, and to approach a faculty group working on data science about research openings. Internships in the same area are usually advertised through the careers office.

## Reading the result

The baseline is fluent, plausible and useless in a specific way: it names no course code, cites nothing, checks no prerequisite and reads no application window, because it has no source to do any of that from. A student cannot verify a single sentence of it. That is exactly the failure mode the proposal set out to remove, and it is the reason the retrieval layer is not optional.

The honest caveat: this compares retrieval against *no evidence at all*, not against a strong retrieval baseline. It shows that the evidence layer is load-bearing; it does not show that this particular BM25 index is the best way to retrieve, and it does not measure answer fluency, which is the axis where a language model would win.
